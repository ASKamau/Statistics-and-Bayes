Reading the notation

(8 points) \(x = (3,\ 5,\ 2,\ 6)\), \(n = 4\).

(a) \[ \sum_{i=1}^{4} x_i = 3 + 5 + 2 + 6 = 16 \qquad\qquad \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{4} x_i = \frac{16}{4} = 4 \]

(b) \[ \sum_{i=1}^{4} 3x_i = 3(3) + 3(5) + 3(2) + 3(6) = 9 + 15 + 6 + 18 = 48 = 3 \times 16 \]

This illustrates that a constant factors out of a sum: \(\sum_i c\,x_i = c\sum_i x_i\).

(c) Square first, then add: \[ \sum_{i=1}^{4} x_i^2 = 3^2 + 5^2 + 2^2 + 6^2 = 9 + 25 + 4 + 36 = 74 \]

(d) Add first, then square: \[ \left(\sum_{i=1}^{4} x_i\right)^2 = (3 + 5 + 2 + 6)^2 = 16^2 = 256 \]

  1. \(\neq\) (d). The sum of squares is not the square of the sum.

(e) \[ \sum_{i=1}^{4} (x_i - \bar{x}) = (3-4) + (5-4) + (2-4) + (6-4) = -1 + 1 - 2 + 2 = 0 \]

The surprise: deviations from the mean always sum to exactly zero. This isn’t a coincidence of these numbers. It holds for any data set, because \(\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum_i x_i - n\bar{x} = n\bar{x} - n\bar{x} = 0\).

(f) \(\mu\) is the population mean, a fixed but usually unknown number describing the whole distribution. \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean, computed from the data we actually have and used to estimate \(\mu\). It changes from sample to sample.

Grading (8 pts): (a) 1.5 · (b) 1.5 · (c) 1 · (d) 1 · (e) 1.5 · (f) 1.5. Sums must be written out term by term; a bare final number earns half credit. In (b) the student must name the rule, not just observe that 48 is three times 16. In (e), a student who notes that the result is always zero (with or without the algebra) earns full credit; the algebra is a bonus. In (f), the key words are population vs. sample, or fixed parameter vs. estimate from data.

The distribution of one random variable

(12 points)

(a) Margins.

Row sums: \(0.10 + 0.14 = 0.24\), \(0.20 + 0.10 = 0.30\), \(0.26 + 0.06 = 0.32\), \(0.12 + 0.02 = 0.14\). Column sums: \(0.10 + 0.20 + 0.26 + 0.12 = 0.68\) and \(0.14 + 0.10 + 0.06 + 0.02 = 0.32\).

Checks: \(0.24 + 0.30 + 0.32 + 0.14 = 1.00\) and \(0.68 + 0.32 = 1.00\).

(b) \[ E(X) = \mu_X = \sum_i x_i\,P(X = x_i) = 0(0.24) + 1(0.30) + 2(0.32) + 3(0.14) = 0 + 0.30 + 0.64 + 0.42 = 1.36 \]

The expected number of branches in an applicant’s tract is 1.36.

(c) \[ E(X^2) = \sum_i x_i^2\,P(X = x_i) = 0^2(0.24) + 1^2(0.30) + 2^2(0.32) + 3^2(0.14) = 0 + 0.30 + 1.28 + 1.26 = 2.84 \]

(d) \[ \text{var}(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2 = 2.84 - (1.36)^2 = 2.84 - 1.8496 = 0.9904 \] \[ \sigma_X = \sqrt{0.9904} \approx 0.9952 \]

(e) By definition, \[ E(Y) = \sum_y y\,P(Y = y) = 0 \cdot P(Y = 0) + 1 \cdot P(Y = 1) = P(Y = 1) \] The \(y = 0\) term vanishes, so the mean of a 0/1 variable is the probability it equals 1. Here, \(E(Y) = P(Y = 1) = \mathbf{0.32}\): 32% of applications are denied.

Grading (12 pts): (a) 3 · (b) 2 · (c) 2 · (d) 3 · (e) 2.

    1. All six margins are required, plus both “sums to 1” checks.
    1. The classic error is squaring the probabilities, or computing \([E(X)]^2 = 1.8496\) and calling it \(E(X^2)\). Either one costs 2 pts.
    1. Carry the error forward. If the student’s (c) is wrong but (d) applies the formula correctly to it, give full credit for (d). A negative variance is a red flag the student should catch; if they catch it, give credit.
    1. The derivation must show the \(0 \cdot P(Y=0)\) term disappearing. A bare “0.32” earns 1 pt.

Two random variables together

(12 points)

(a) \[ P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = \frac{P(X = 0,\, Y = 1)}{P(X = 0)} = \frac{0.14}{0.24} \approx 0.5833 \] \[ P(Y = 1 \mid X = 3) = \frac{P(X = 3,\, Y = 1)}{P(X = 3)} = \frac{0.02}{0.14} \approx 0.1429 \]

(b) The overall denial rate is 0.32. Applicants in tracts with no branches are denied 58% of the time, well above average. Applicants in tracts with three branches are denied only about 14% of the time, well below average. Denial and branch presence are therefore not independent: knowing \(X\) changes the probability of denial. (If they were independent, both conditional probabilities would equal 0.32.)

(c) Since \(Y^2 = Y\), \(E(Y^2) = E(Y) = 0.32\): \[ \text{var}(Y) = E(Y^2) - [E(Y)]^2 = 0.32 - (0.32)^2 = 0.32 - 0.1024 = 0.2176 \] \[ \sigma_Y = \sqrt{0.2176} \approx 0.4665 \]

(Equivalently, \(\text{var}(Y) = p(1-p) = 0.32 \times 0.68 = 0.2176\), which is the form used in the R code.)

(d) Only cells with \(x \neq 0\) and \(y = 1\) contribute: \[ \begin{aligned} E(XY) &= \sum_x \sum_y x\,y\,P(X = x,\, Y = y) \\ &= (1)(1)(0.10) + (2)(1)(0.06) + (3)(1)(0.02) \\ &= 0.10 + 0.12 + 0.06 = 0.28 \end{aligned} \]

(e) \[ \text{cov}(X,Y) = E(XY) - \mu_X\mu_Y = 0.28 - (1.36)(0.32) = 0.28 - 0.4352 = -0.1552 \] \[ \rho_{XY} = \frac{\text{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y} = \frac{-0.1552}{(0.9952)(0.4665)} = \frac{-0.1552}{0.4642} \approx -0.3343 \]

(f) The negative sign means that applicants in tracts with more bank branches tend to be denied less often: as \(X\) goes up, \(Y\) tends to go down. This number does not establish causation. Opening a branch would not necessarily lower denial rates. Tracts with more branches may differ in income, credit histories, wealth, or racial composition, and those differences could drive both branch location and denial. Correlation also captures only the linear part of the relationship.

Grading (12 pts): (a) 2 · (b) 2 · (c) 2 · (d) 2 · (e) 3 · (f) 1.

    1. The most common error is dividing by \(P(Y=1)\) instead of \(P(X=x)\) (e.g., \(0.14/0.32 = 0.4375\)). That is \(P(X=0 \mid Y=1)\), a different question. Deduct 1 per part.
    1. Full credit requires comparing both numbers to 0.32 and drawing a conclusion (dependence). “Independence” is not required vocabulary.
    1. Accept \(\rho\) between \(-0.3342\) and \(-0.3345\) (rounding of \(\sigma_X\), \(\sigma_Y\)). Carry errors forward from (b)–(d).
    1. Half for the sign interpretation, half for “not causal” (or “linear only”) with a reason. “Correlation isn’t causation” without a reason or example earns half of that half.

Discussion point for Tuesday: this table is a stylized version of the branch-desert / credit-access pattern, a natural bridge to omitted-variable bias later in the course.
